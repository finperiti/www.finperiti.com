Why you’re in scope

Credit institutions have been obliged entities under EU anti-money laundering law for decades, and they are first on the list in Article 3 of Regulation (EU) 2024/1624 (the EU AML Regulation).

Financial intelligence unit: .

Financial-sector supervisor: .

Lawyers, accountants, estate agents and dealers are usually supervised by their professional body or another national authority, so check which applies to you. Your supervisor’s guidance sets out any extra national requirements.

Your AML supervisor: The Central Bank of Ireland.

Your AML supervisor: The FIAU, with the Malta Financial Services Authority (MFSA) as your licensing authority.

UK rules: In the UK, your AML duties come from the Money Laundering Regulations 2017.

Your AML supervisor: The Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) and, for banks, the Prudential Regulation Authority.

UAE rules: In the UAE, your AML duties come from Federal Decree-Law No. 10 of 2025 and its Executive Regulations.

Your AML supervisor: The Central Bank of the UAE (CBUAE). If you’re licensed in the DIFC, it’s the DFSA, and in the ADGM, the FSRA.

What it means for you

  • Your national rules are replaced by one directly applicable EU rulebook, so policies written for your local law need updating to the Regulation.
  • The largest cross-border groups can be selected for direct supervision by the new EU Anti-Money Laundering Authority (AMLA) from 2028.
  • Cross-border correspondent relationships keep their enhanced due diligence requirements, and shell banks remain prohibited.

Like every obliged entity, you will need to:

  • Know your client. Identify and verify every client before you take them on, and the people who own or control them: anyone with 25% or more, or control by other means.
  • Assess the risk. Keep a written risk assessment of your business, rate each client’s risk, and apply enhanced checks to higher risk clients, politically exposed persons and high risk countries.
  • Screen and monitor. Check clients against sanctions and PEP lists at onboarding, and keep monitoring them for as long as you work together.
  • Policies and people. Have written policies and controls, a compliance manager at management level, and staff who are trained to spot problems.
  • Report and keep records. Report suspicious activity to your national Financial Intelligence Unit without tipping off the client, and keep records for five years after a relationship ends.

What you need to do

  1. Map every entity, branch and product in your group against the Regulation, and compare your current policies with the new requirements.
  2. Update your business wide risk assessment, policies and procedures to the Regulation, rather than your current national rules.
  3. Appoint a compliance manager at management level, and train your staff.
  4. Put a client onboarding process in place that verifies identity and beneficial owners, screens for sanctions and PEPs, and records every decision.
  5. Bring existing clients up to the new standard on a risk basis, starting with the highest risk ones, ahead of 10 July 2027.
